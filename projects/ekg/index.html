Build 01 · May 2026

Electrocardiogram

An analog front end that pulls a heartbeat out of the noise. The signal at the skin is a few millivolts sitting under interference many times larger, so the circuit amplifies by 600× while throwing away everything that isn't a heartbeat.

01Overview

Built for ENGR40M at Stanford. An ECG is a hard analog problem disguised as a simple one: the electrical activity of the heart shows up at the skin as only a few millivolts, riding on top of mains hum, muscle activity and DC drift that can be orders of magnitude larger. Amplify naively and you amplify the interference just as faithfully.

The circuit is two stages. An instrumentation amplifier rejects everything common to both electrodes and amplifies only the difference between them, then a bandpass filter keeps the narrow band where a heartbeat lives and discards the rest.

02What is inside

  1. 01INA126P instrumentation amplifier (on breakout)
  2. 02MCP601 operational amplifier
  3. 03RG gain-setting resistor
  4. 04100 kΩ and 10 kΩ resistors
  5. 0547 µF and 0.1 µF capacitors
  6. 06Analog Discovery 2 (supplies, signal generator, oscilloscope)
  7. 07Breadboard

03How it was built

  1. 1

    Instrumentation amplifier stage

    An INA126P amplifies the difference between its two inputs while rejecting anything common to both - which is exactly what mains hum is, since it couples into both electrodes almost equally. A single resistor RG sets the gain. Driving the stage with a 5 mV test pulse gave a 0.7 V output, an empirical gain of 140.

  2. 2

    Bandpass filter stage

    The second stage is built around an MCP601 op-amp with 100 kΩ / 0.1 µF and 10 kΩ / 47 µF pairs setting the corners, giving a passband of roughly 0.34 Hz to 15.92 Hz and a further gain of 10. The low corner kills the DC offset and slow baseline wander from electrodes shifting on skin; the high corner removes muscle noise and mains interference well above the heartbeat.

  3. 3

    Measured performance

    End to end the circuit turns a 1 mV input into a 0.6 V output - a combined gain of 600, or about 55 dB, matching the 140 × 10 the two stages predict. Feeding a DC signal in confirmed the filter is doing its job: the output barely moves, because DC sits at 0 Hz and falls outside the passband entirely.

  4. 4

    Bench setup and safety

    An Analog Discovery 2 does three jobs at once here: it generates the ±2.5 V rails, produces the 5 mV 1 Hz pulse standing in for a heartbeat, and acts as the oscilloscope. Measuring a real person adds requirements that have nothing to do with signal quality - current-limiting resistors, an isolation board separating the circuit from the laptop, and running the laptop on battery so nothing is tied to mains.

04What was tricky

  • ·Noise is still visible at the output even after filtering, and the obvious fix makes things worse. Lowering the upper corner - by raising the 10 kΩ resistor or the 0.1 µF capacitor - would cut more high-frequency noise, but a heartbeat is a sharp spike rather than a smooth sine, so it needs that bandwidth to hold its shape. Filter too aggressively and the QRS complex rounds off and flattens.
  • ·Electrodes on skin drift, and any DC offset gets multiplied by the full gain of the chain. The high-pass corner has to sit low enough to pass a ~1 Hz heartbeat while still blocking that wander.
  • ·Measuring a real person changes the requirements entirely: current-limiting resistors, an isolation board between the circuit and the laptop, and running the laptop on battery so the subject is never referenced to mains.

05What I learned

  • 01

    Amplification and rejection are separate problems. An instrumentation amplifier is valuable not because it has high gain, but because it amplifies only the difference between its inputs - mains hum couples into both electrodes almost equally, so treating it as common-mode is what removes it.

  • 02

    Gain multiplies across stages, and so does anything you failed to remove. 140 in the first stage and 10 in the second gives 600 overall, which is also 600× on any offset that reached the input.

  • 03

    A filter is a statement about which frequencies carry your signal. Feeding DC into the finished circuit barely moved the output - DC is 0 Hz and falls outside the passband - which made the abstraction concrete in a way the maths alone did not.

  • 04

    Filtering is a trade, not a free improvement. Every decibel of extra noise rejection costs signal fidelity when the thing you are measuring is a sharp transient.
